# Backbite reconfiguration of Hamiltonian paths: the minimum-degree threshold is asymptotically $n/2$

*Efe Çam — with AI assistance (see "How this was produced" at the end). Version 1, 26 November 2026.*

## Abstract

A *backbite* on a Hamiltonian path $w_1w_2\cdots w_n$ of a graph $G$ chooses an endpoint, say $w_1$, and an index $k\ge 3$ with $w_1w_k\in E(G)$, adds the edge $w_1w_k$ and deletes $w_{k-1}w_k$. The result $w_{k-1}\cdots w_1w_k\cdots w_n$ is again a Hamiltonian path. Let $R(G)$ be the graph on the Hamiltonian paths of $G$ (a path and its reversal being identified) in which two paths are adjacent when a single backbite carries one to the other. Kazazi [K] proved that $R(G)$ is connected whenever $\delta(G)\ge\lceil (2n-1)/3\rceil$. For every $n\ge 8$ he constructed graphs with $\delta(G)=\lfloor n/2\rfloor-3$ and disconnected $R(G)$, and he conjectured that $\delta(G)\ge n/2$ suffices (Conjecture 7.8 of [K]).

We prove the conjecture asymptotically:

> **Theorem 1.** For every $\gamma>0$ there is $n_0(\gamma)$ such that every graph $G$ on $n\ge n_0(\gamma)$ vertices with $\delta(G)\ge(1/2+\gamma)n$ has a connected backbite reconfiguration graph $R(G)$.

Together with Kazazi's construction, the minimum-degree threshold for connectivity of $R(G)$ is therefore $n/2+o(n)$. The main new tool is a *vertex-extension lemma* (Lemma 4). It says that connectivity of $R$ survives adding one vertex of large degree to a graph of minimum degree a little above half its order. We also report computations:

- Conjecture 7.8 holds for all $n\le 11$.
- A stronger "fixed-endpoint" (Pósa-rotation) version holds for all $n\le 10$ and fails just below the Dirac threshold.
- Hamiltonicity alone does not imply connectivity of $R(G)$.
- A natural Bondy–Chvátal-type closure approach fails.

## 1. Definitions and elementary facts

All graphs are finite and simple. For a Hamiltonian path $P=w_1\cdots w_n$ and $3\le k\le n$ with $w_1w_k\in E(G)$, write $\mathrm{bb}(w_1,w_k)$ for the backbite at the endpoint $w_1$ with target $w_k$. By definition $\mathrm{bb}(w_1,w_k)$ produces $w_{k-1}\cdots w_1w_k\cdots w_n$. Backbites at the other end are symmetric.

Three facts are immediate from the definition:

- A backbite at one endpoint never moves the other endpoint. This holds even when the target is the other endpoint.
- A backbite is invertible: $\mathrm{bb}(w_{k-1},w_k)$ undoes $\mathrm{bb}(w_1,w_k)$.
- A backbite deletes exactly one edge and adds exactly one edge.

**Cycles.** Let $C$ be a Hamiltonian cycle and $xy\in E(C)$. In the path $C-xy$, the backbite $\mathrm{bb}(x,y)$ adds $xy$ and deletes the other edge of $C$ at $y$. Repeating such *rotations* moves the missing edge around $C$. Hence all $n$ paths $C-e$, $e\in E(C)$, lie in one component of $R(G)$. Two Hamiltonian cycles are called **equivalent** if their edge-deletions lie in the same component of $R(G)$.

**Fact 2 (crossings).** Let $P=w_1\cdots w_h$ be a Hamiltonian path of a graph $F$ on $h$ vertices whose endpoints $a=w_1$ and $b=w_h$ are non-adjacent. Then there are at least $d_F(a)+d_F(b)-(h-1)$ indices $i$ with $aw_{i+1},\,bw_i\in E(F)$.

For each such index, $2\le i\le h-2$. The backbite $\mathrm{bb}(a,w_{i+1})$ deletes $w_iw_{i+1}$ and yields a path with adjacent endpoints $w_i,b$. Symmetrically, $\mathrm{bb}(b,w_i)$ keeps $a$ fixed and yields a path with adjacent endpoints $a,w_{i+1}$. In both cases the deleted edge is $w_iw_{i+1}$.

*Proof.* Let $S=\{i: aw_{i+1}\in E\}$ and $T=\{i: bw_i\in E\}$. Both are subsets of $\{1,\dots,h-1\}$, with $|S|=d_F(a)$ and $|T|=d_F(b)$, so $|S\cap T|\ge|S|+|T|-(h-1)$. Non-adjacency of $a$ and $b$ excludes $i=1$ and $i=h-1$. $\square$

In particular, if $d(x)+d(y)\ge n$ for all non-adjacent $x,y$ (Ore's condition), then every Hamiltonian path is cyclic (has adjacent endpoints) or is one backbite away from a cyclic path. So under Ore's condition, $R(G)$ is connected if and only if all Hamiltonian cycles are equivalent.

**Fact 3 (interval reversal).** Let a Hamiltonian cycle contain the segment $a,u_1,\dots,u_t,b$ with $t\ge 2$, and suppose $au_t,\,u_1b\in E(G)$. Then the cycle obtained by reversing $u_1\cdots u_t$ is equivalent to the original.

*Proof.* Delete $au_1$. This leaves a path from $u_1$ to $a$ which, read from $a$, ends $\ldots,b,u_t,\ldots,u_1$. The backbite $\mathrm{bb}(a,u_t)$ deletes $bu_t$ and adds $au_t$. Its result has endpoints $b,u_1$, and closing it with the edge $u_1b$ gives the reversed cycle. $\square$

**Forcing graphs.** A graph $J$ is **forcing** if:

- (i) $R(J)$ is connected; and
- (ii) from every ordering (permutation) of $V(J)$, regarded as a Hamiltonian path of the complete graph on $V(J)$, some sequence of backbites, each adding an edge of $J$, reaches a Hamiltonian path of $J$.

**Lemma 1.** If $G$ contains a forcing graph $J$ as a spanning subgraph, then $R(G)$ is connected.

*Proof.* Let $P$ be a Hamiltonian path of $G$. A backbite that adds an edge of $J\subseteq G$ produces a Hamiltonian path of $G$. By (ii), $P$ is therefore joined in $R(G)$ to a Hamiltonian path of $J$. By (i), all Hamiltonian paths of $J$ are joined in $R(J)\subseteq R(G)$. $\square$

Kazazi's theorem [K, Thm 1.1] says that the square of a Hamiltonian path is forcing. We need a forcing graph that a dense graph contains on a bounded number of vertices. Its edge set is a blow-up of a short path ending in a triangle.

## 2. A forcing blow-up

For $k\ge4$, let $T_k$ be the path $1,2,\dots,k$ together with the edge $\{k-2,k\}$. Let $s_1,\dots,s_k$ be positive integers. The graph $J=J(s_1,\dots,s_k)$ is defined as follows:

- each vertex $i$ of $T_k$ is replaced by an independent set $W_i$ with $|W_i|=s_i$;
- each edge $ij$ of $T_k$ is replaced by the complete bipartite graph between $W_i$ and $W_j$.

Vertices in the same class $W_i$ have identical neighbourhoods ("twins").

**Lemma 2.** If $1\le s_1<s_2<\cdots<s_{k-2}<s_{k-1}=s_k$, then $J(s_1,\dots,s_k)$ is forcing.

*Proof.* **Property (ii).** Consider an arbitrary ordering of $V(J)$. Call an edge of it *bad* if it is not an edge of $J$, and let $\beta$ be the number of bad edges. We use only backbites adding edges of $J$. Such a backbite never increases $\beta$, and it decreases $\beta$ exactly when the deleted edge is bad.

*Advancing.* Fix one endpoint and suppose the other ("free") endpoint $x$ lies in $W_i$ with $i\le k-2$. Read the ordering from $x$.

- Every vertex of $W_{i+1}$ has a predecessor, and distinct vertices have distinct predecessors. Since $s_{i+1}>s_i$, some $y\in W_{i+1}$ has its predecessor $z$ outside $W_i$.
- In particular $y$ is not the second vertex, and $xy\in E(J)$, so $\mathrm{bb}(x,y)$ is a $J$-adding backbite. It deletes $zy$.
- If $zy$ is bad, $\beta$ drops.
- Otherwise $z$ lies in a class $W_m$ with $m$ adjacent to $i+1$ in $T_k$ and $m\ne i$. Every such $m$ exceeds $i$, because $i$ is the smallest neighbour of $i+1$ in $T_k$. The new free endpoint is $z$.

Repeat. Either $\beta$ drops, or after fewer than $k$ steps the free endpoint lies in $W_{k-1}\cup W_k$. Then fix this endpoint, free the other one and repeat. Either $\beta$ drops or both endpoints lie in $W_{k-1}\cup W_k$.

*Closing.* If the endpoints lie in different classes among $W_{k-1},W_k$, they are adjacent in $J$.

Suppose both lie in the same class $L\in\{W_{k-1},W_k\}$, and let $L'$ be the other one. Consider the backbites from one endpoint $x\in L$ with targets in $L'\cup W_{k-2}$; all of these are edges of $J$.

- These $|L'|+s_{k-2}$ targets have distinct predecessors.
- Since $|L'|=|L|$ and $s_{k-2}>s_{k-3}$, there are more targets than vertices in $L\cup W_{k-3}$. So some target $y$ has predecessor $z\notin L\cup W_{k-3}$; in particular $y$ is not the second vertex.
- If $zy$ is bad, $\beta$ drops.
- Otherwise $zy\in E(J)$. Look at the neighbours of $y$ in $T_k$: if $y\in L'$ they are $\{k-2\}$ and the class of $L$; if $y\in W_{k-2}$ they are $k-3,k-1,k$. Since $z\notin L\cup W_{k-3}$, in either case $z\in W_{k-2}\cup L'$.
- Both $W_{k-2}$ and $L'$ are complete to $L$, which contains the other endpoint. The new endpoints are therefore adjacent in $J$.

*Descending.* We have reached an ordering whose endpoints are joined by an edge $e\in E(J)$, unless $\beta$ already dropped. Close it into a cyclic sequence $C$ with the missing edge $e$. If $\beta>0$, rotate $C$, moving the missing edge around, until a bad edge is deleted for the first time. Each rotation adds the previously missing edge, which is $e$ or an edge deleted earlier, hence an edge of $J$. At the first deletion of a bad edge, $\beta$ drops.

Iterating gives $\beta=0$, i.e. a Hamiltonian path of $J$. This proves (ii). Applied to Hamiltonian paths of $J$ themselves, the same procedure (Advancing and Closing, all moves inside $J$) shows that every Hamiltonian path of $J$ is joined in $R(J)$ to an edge-deletion of a Hamiltonian cycle of $J$.

**Property (i): all Hamiltonian cycles of $J$ are equivalent.** For a Hamiltonian cycle $C$ of $J$ and $ij\in E(T_k)$, let $m_{ij}$ be the number of edges of $C$ between $W_i$ and $W_j$. Since every vertex of $W_i$ has two cycle edges, $\sum_{j\sim i}m_{ij}=2s_i$. Along the path $1,\dots,k-2$ these equations determine $m_{12},m_{23},\dots,m_{k-3,k-2}$ successively. The three remaining equations prescribe the three pairwise sums of the triangle multiplicities, hence determine them. So the multiplicities do not depend on $C$.

Projecting $C$ onto the classes gives an Euler circuit of a fixed multigraph $M$ on $V(T_k)$ with $m_{ij}$ parallel edges $ij$. Label the parallel edges and assign labels to traversals arbitrarily.

*Claim.* Any two Euler trails of a multigraph with the same start, the same end and the same labelled edges are related by reversals of closed subtrails.

*Proof of the claim.* Let the desired first edge be $e=xy$, traversed from $x$ to $y$.

- If the current trail traverses $e$ from $y$ to $x$, the prefix ending with $e$ is a closed trail at $x$; reversing it makes the trail start with $e$ in the right direction.
- If the trail already starts with $e$ in the right direction, nothing needs to be done.
- Otherwise the trail is $A\,e\,B$, where $A$ is a non-empty closed trail at $x$ and $B$ starts at $y$. In the target trail, the edges of $A\cup B$ form a single trail from $y$, so $V(A)\cap V(B)\neq\emptyset$ (when $B$ is empty, read $V(B)=\{y\}$). Pick a common vertex $z$ and split $A=A_1A_2$, $B=B_1B_2$ at $z$. Reversing the closed trail $A_2eB_1$ makes $e$ traversed towards $x$, which is the first case.

Recurse on the suffix after $e$. $\square$

Each reversal of a proper closed subtrail can be realised up to equivalence:

- The subtrail runs between two occurrences of the same class $W_i$. These are distinct cycle vertices $x,y\in W_i$.
- Reversing the cycle interval strictly between them changes exactly the two boundary adjacencies, and the new adjacencies exist because $x$ and $y$ are twins. Fact 3 applies (intervals of length $\le 1$ are trivial).

So any Hamiltonian cycle of $J$ is equivalent to one with any prescribed cyclic class sequence of $M$. Finally, two vertices $x,y$ of the same class can be swapped while keeping the class sequence:

- Write the cycle as $x\,U\,y\,V$.
- Reverse $xUy$, then reverse the interior $U^R$.
- Both reversals have their boundary edges because $x$ and $y$ are twins, so Fact 3 applies to each.

Hence all Hamiltonian cycles of $J$ are equivalent. Together with the last sentence of the proof of (ii), this gives (i). $\square$

## 3. Vertex extension

**Lemma 4 (vertex extension).** Let $H$ be a graph on $h$ vertices with $\delta(H)\ge h/2+2$ and $R(H)$ connected. Let $G$ be obtained by adding a vertex $v$ with $d:=|N(v)\cap V(H)|\ge h/2+2$. Then $R(G)$ is connected.

*Proof.* **Attachments.** Let $D$ be a Hamiltonian cycle of $H$. An *attachment* to $D$ at $a$ is a path $v,a,\dots,b$ with the following properties:

- $va\in E(G)$;
- $b$ is a neighbour of $a$ on $D$;
- the part $a,\dots,b$ traverses $D$ from $a$ to $b$ omitting the edge $ab$.

All attachments to $D$ lie in one component of $R(G)$:

- *Same attachment point, other direction.* In $(v,a,\dots,b)$, the backbite from $b$ with target $a$ (an edge of $D$) deletes the edge between $a$ and its other $D$-neighbour. It produces the attachment at $a$ in the other direction.
- *Moving to another attachment point $c\ne a$.* Let $c\in N(v)$. Writing the attachment at $a$ as $(v,a,A,c,B,b)$ (where $A,B$ may be empty), the backbite from $v$ with target $c$ gives $(A^R,a,v,c,B,b)$. Then the backbite from $b$ with target $a$ (the edge $ab$ of $D$) gives $(v,c,B,b,a,A)$. This is an attachment at $c$.
- *The case $c=b$.* The same two moves turn $(v,a,A,b)$ into $(A^R,a,v,b)$ and then into $(v,b,a,A)$.

For every edge $xy$ of $D$ with $x,y\in N(v)$, let $D_{xy}$ be the Hamiltonian cycle of $G$ obtained by inserting $v$ between $x$ and $y$. Deleting $vy$ from $D_{xy}$ gives an attachment at $x$. So all these cycles are equivalent to the attachments of $D$; call this component the *attachment component of $D$*.

**Lifts.** Let $P$ be a Hamiltonian path of $H$.

- An edge of $P$ is *eligible* if both its ends lie in $N(v)$. Inserting $v$ into an eligible edge $e$ gives a Hamiltonian path $P_e$ of $G$, a *lift* of $P$.
- The $d$ neighbours of $v$ form at most $h-d+1$ runs along $P$. Hence $P$ has at least $d-(h-d+1)=2d-h-1\ge 3$ eligible edges.

*(a) All lifts of $P$ lie in one component.*

- If the endpoints of $P$ are adjacent, then $D=P+(\text{endpoint edge})$ is a Hamiltonian cycle of $H$. Each lift $P_e$ is an edge-deletion of $D_e$, so all lifts lie in the attachment component of $D$.
- Otherwise, let $e,f$ be eligible. By Fact 2 in $H$ there are at least $(h/2+2)+(h/2+2)-(h-1)=5$ crossing indices, each deleting a different edge of $P$. Choose one whose deleted edge is neither $e$ nor $f$.
  - The corresponding backbite $P\to P'$ is also a valid backbite $P_e\to P'_e$ and $P_f\to P'_f$: the target and its predecessor are unchanged, since $v$ sits in a retained edge.
  - $P'$ has adjacent endpoints, so by the first case $P'_e$ and $P'_f$ are in one component, and hence so are $P_e$ and $P_f$.

*(b) Lifts of $R(H)$-adjacent paths are joined.* Let $P\to Q$ be a backbite in $H$ at an endpoint $p$ with target $t$; it deletes the edge between $t$ and its predecessor $t^-$. Since $P$ has at least three eligible edges and only one edge is deleted, some eligible edge $e\ne t^-t$ survives. The same backbite is valid on $P_e$ and gives $Q_e$:

- $t$ is at position at least $3$ in $P_e$, because inserting $v$ only increases positions;
- the predecessor of $t$ in $P_e$ is still $t^-$, because $e\ne t^-t$;
- if $e$ lies in the reversed prefix, $v$ moves with it and stays between the ends of $e$. Since $R(H)$ is connected, (a) and (b) put all lifts of all Hamiltonian paths of $H$ in a single component $\mathcal K$ of $R(G)$.

**Every path reaches $\mathcal K$.** We have $\delta(G)\ge h/2+2>(h+1)/2$. Let $Q$ be any Hamiltonian path of $G$.

1. By Fact 2 (in $G$), $Q$ is joined to an edge-deletion of a Hamiltonian cycle $C$ of $G$.
2. Rotating, we reach $C$ minus an edge at $v$: a path $(v,a,\dots,b)$ whose part $a\cdots b$ is a Hamiltonian path of $H$.
3. If $ab\notin E$, Fact 2 in $H$ (at least five crossings) gives a backbite from the $b$-end, keeping $a$ fixed. It turns $a\cdots b$ into a Hamiltonian path of $H$ with adjacent endpoints and does not touch $v$. We now have an attachment to a Hamiltonian cycle $D$ of $H$.
4. Since $|N(v)\cap V(D)|=d>h/2$, two neighbours $x,y$ of $v$ are consecutive on $D$. The cycle $D_{xy}$ is in the attachment component of $D$.
5. Deleting from $D_{xy}$ any edge $g$ not incident with $v$ gives the lift $(D-g)_{xy}\in\mathcal K$.

Hence $R(G)=\mathcal K$ is connected. $\square$

## 4. A typical core

We use two standard tools.

**Hoeffding [H, §6].** Let $X$ be the number of marked elements in a uniform random sample without replacement of size $q\ge 1$ from a finite population. Then $\Pr[X\le\mathbb E X-u]\le e^{-2u^2/q}$.

**Erdős [E].** For all $k,L$ and $c>0$ there is $n_1$ such that, for $n\ge n_1$, every family of at least $cn^k$ ordered $k$-tuples from $[n]^k$ contains a box $D_1\times\cdots\times D_k$ with $|D_i|=L$ for all $i$. This is the statement that complete $k$-partite $k$-graphs have Turán density $0$, applied to the $k$-partite $k$-graph on $k$ copies of $[n]$.

If all tuples in the family have pairwise distinct coordinates, the sets $D_i$ are pairwise disjoint.

Fix $0<\alpha\le 1/32$.

**Lemma 5.** Let $G$ have $n$ vertices and $\delta(G)\ge(1/2+16\alpha)n$, and let $s^*$ be any constant. For $n$ sufficiently large (depending on $\alpha,s^*$) there is $S\subseteq V(G)$ with the following properties:

- $s^*\le|S|=s\le s^{**}(\alpha,s^*)$;
- $R(G[S])$ is connected;
- $d_S(x)\ge(1/2+8\alpha)s$ for every $x\in S$;
- $d_S(x)\ge(1/2+8\alpha)s$ for all but at most $\alpha n/2$ vertices $x\in V(G)$.

*Proof.* **Good tuples.** Let $k$ be large (depending on $\alpha$). Call an ordered $k$-tuple of distinct vertices with underlying set $Q$ *good* if:

- $\delta(G[Q])\ge(1/2+12\alpha)k$; and
- at most $\alpha^2n$ vertices $x$ of $G$ have $d_Q(x)<(1/2+12\alpha)k$.

We show that a uniformly random such tuple is good with probability at least $1/2$.

- *First condition.* Condition on the vertex $x$ in a fixed position. The other $k-1$ entries form a uniform sample from $V\setminus\{x\}$, in which a proportion of at least $1/2+16\alpha$ are neighbours of $x$. So $\mathbb E\,d_Q(x)\ge(1/2+16\alpha)(k-1)\ge(1/2+12\alpha)k+3\alpha k$ for $k\ge 1/\alpha$. Hoeffding bounds the failure probability by $e^{-18\alpha^2k}$. Taking a union bound over the $k$ positions gives less than $1/4$.
- *Second condition.* For each fixed $x$, $\mathbb E\,d_Q(x)=d(x)k/n\ge(1/2+16\alpha)k$, so $\Pr[d_Q(x)<(1/2+12\alpha)k]\le e^{-32\alpha^2k}$. By Markov's inequality the probability of more than $\alpha^2n$ such $x$ is at most $e^{-32\alpha^2k}/\alpha^2<1/4$.

Hence, for large $n$, at least $n^k/4$ ordered tuples are good.

**A box.** By pigeonhole, some labelled graph $F$ on $[k]$ is induced (position-wise) by at least $c\,n^k$ good tuples, where $c=2^{-\binom k2}/4$. By Erdős's theorem with $L$ fixed below, there are pairwise disjoint $D_1,\dots,D_k$, $|D_i|=L$, such that every transversal is a good tuple inducing $F$. In particular:

- $\delta(F)\ge(1/2+12\alpha)k$;
- $G[D_i,D_j]$ is complete bipartite whenever $ij\in E(F)$;
- every transversal is good.

**$F$ contains $T_k$ (relabelling).** Take an edge $ab$ of $F$. Since $\delta(F)>k/2$, $a$ and $b$ have a common neighbour $c$. The graph $F-\{b,c\}$ has minimum degree at least $\delta(F)-2\ge(k-2)/2$ (as $12\alpha k\ge 1$). By Dirac's theorem it has a Hamiltonian cycle; delete a cycle edge at $a$ to get a Hamiltonian path ending at $a$, then append $b,c$. This path together with the edge $ac$ is a copy of $T_k$ in $F$. Relabel so that it is $T_k$ itself.

**Choice of $S$.** Let $t\ge\max(k/\alpha,s^*)$ and put $s_i=t+i$ for $i<k$ and $s_k=t+k-1$; take $L=t+k$. Choose $S_i\subseteq D_i$ with $|S_i|=s_i$ and let $S=\bigcup S_i$, so $s^*\le s=\sum s_i\le k(t+k)$.

- $G[S]$ contains $J(s_1,\dots,s_k)$ as a spanning subgraph. By Lemmas 1 and 2, $R(G[S])$ is connected.
- For $x\in S_i$: $d_S(x)\ge d_F(i)\min_j s_j\ge(1/2+12\alpha)k(t+1)$. Since $(t+1)/(t+k)\ge 1-\alpha$, this is at least $(1/2+8\alpha)s$.

**Most outside vertices are typical.** Let $p(x)=\frac1k\sum_i d_{S_i}(x)/s_i$. For a random transversal $Q'$ with independent uniform $y_i\in S_i$, we have $\mathbb E\,d_{Q'}(x)=k\,p(x)$. Let $\pi_x=\Pr[d_{Q'}(x)<(1/2+12\alpha)k]$. Then $p(x)\ge(1-\pi_x)(1/2+12\alpha)$.

- If $p(x)<1/2+10\alpha$, then $\pi_x\ge 2\alpha$ (using $\alpha\le 1/24$).
- Every transversal is good, so $\sum_x\pi_x\le\alpha^2n$. Hence at most $\alpha n/2$ vertices have $p(x)<1/2+10\alpha$.
- For all other $x$: $d_S(x)\ge k\,p(x)\min_i s_i\ge(1/2+10\alpha)k(t+1)\ge(1/2+10\alpha)(1-\alpha)s\ge(1/2+8\alpha)s$. $\square$

## 5. Proof of Theorem 1

We may assume $\gamma\le 1/2$; put $\alpha=\gamma/16$. Choose $s^*$ such that both
$$4\alpha s^*\ge 3 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \sum_{j\ge 1}(s+j+1)e^{-32\alpha^2(s+j)}<1 \ \text{ for all } s\ge s^*.$$
Let $S$ be given by Lemma 5, and let $n$ be large enough that $s\le\alpha n/2$.

Put:

- $B=\{x\notin S: d_S(x)<(1/2+8\alpha)s\}$, so $|B|\le\alpha n/2$;
- $U=V\setminus(S\cup B)$, with $M=|U|$.

For every $x\in V$ we then have $d_U(x)\ge(1/2+16\alpha)n-s-|B|\ge(1/2+14\alpha)n\ge(1/2+14\alpha)M$.

**A good order.** Order $U$ uniformly at random as $u_1,\dots,u_M$ and let $U_j=\{u_1,\dots,u_j\}$. We claim that with positive probability, for all $0\le j<M$,
$$d_{S\cup U_j}(x)\ \ge\ \frac{s+j}2+2\qquad\text{for every }x\in S\cup U_{j+1}. \tag{1}$$

- **$j=0$.** This holds because $d_S(x)\ge(1/2+8\alpha)s\ge s/2+2$ for $x\in S\cup U$ (as $8\alpha s\ge 6$).
- **$x\in S$, $j\ge1$.** $d_{U_j}(x)$ is a sample count with mean at least $(1/2+14\alpha)j$.
- **$x=u_r$, $r\le j+1$, $j\ge1$.** Conditioned on this, $U_j\setminus\{x\}$ is a uniform sample of size $j-1$ or $j$ from $U\setminus\{x\}$, a population with neighbour proportion at least $1/2+14\alpha$. So the mean is at least $(1/2+14\alpha)j-1$. If the sample is empty, the count is deterministic and the bound below holds directly.
- **The tail bound.** In all cases the mean of $d_{S\cup U_j}(x)$ exceeds the right side of (1) by at least $8\alpha s+14\alpha j-3\ge 4\alpha(s+j)$. Hoeffding bounds the failure probability by $\exp(-32\alpha^2(s+j)^2/j)\le e^{-32\alpha^2(s+j)}$.
- **Union bound.** Summing over the $s+j+1$ choices of vertex or position and over $j\ge 1$, the total failure probability is less than $1$ by the choice of $s^*$.

Fix such an order.

**Adding $U$.** Let $H_j=G[S\cup U_j]$. We know $R(H_0)$ is connected. By (1), $\delta(H_j)\ge|H_j|/2+2$ and $u_{j+1}$ has at least $|H_j|/2+2$ neighbours in $H_j$. So Lemma 4 gives, inductively, that $R(G[S\cup U])$ is connected.

**Adding $B$.** Add the vertices of $B$ one at a time. If $A$ is the current vertex set, $|V\setminus A|\le\alpha n/2$. Every vertex has at least $(1/2+16\alpha)n-\alpha n\ge|A|/2+2$ neighbours in $A$ for large $n$, so Lemma 4 applies at each step. Hence $R(G)$ is connected. $\square$

**Remarks.**

1. Since $s^*$, $k$, $t$ and $L$ depend only on $\alpha$, the bound $n_0(\gamma)$ is a (very large) function of $\gamma$ alone. Through Erdős's theorem it is tower-type in $1/\gamma$ in the worst case.
2. The method cannot reach the exact threshold. Lemma 4 needs every intermediate induced subgraph to have minimum degree above half its order. This fails for near-bipartite graphs such as $K_{n/2,n/2}$, whose induced subgraphs are bipartite.
3. The constants in Lemma 4 can be lowered slightly: $\sigma_2(H)\ge h+2$ and $d\ge(h+3)/2$ suffice, by the same proof. We do not need this.

## 6. Computational results

All code is in `code/` and all raw outputs are in `data/`. Graphs were generated with `geng` (nauty). $R(G)$ was computed by exhaustive enumeration of Hamiltonian paths with union–find, in C.

1. **Conjecture 7.8 for $n\le 11$.**

   | Class | Graphs | Contain spanning $P_n^2$ (Kazazi) | Checked directly | Result |
   |---|---|---|---|---|
   | $n=10$, $\delta\ge5$ | 108,376 | 107,296 | 1,080 | all connected |
   | $n=11$, $\delta\ge6$ | 868,311 | 868,030 | 281 | all connected |

   Together with earlier checks for $n\le 9$, the conjecture holds for all $n\le 11$.

2. **Far below the threshold.** Every graph with $n=10,\ \delta\ge 4$ (1,249,973 graphs) and every graph with $n=9,\ \delta\ge3$ (84,245 graphs) that has a Hamiltonian path has connected $R(G)$. Every graph satisfying Ore's condition with $n\le 9$ has connected $R(G)$.

3. **Hamiltonicity is not sufficient.** All disconnected examples in [K] are non-Hamiltonian; they have toughness below $1$. Take $C_{10}$ on $0,\dots,9$ together with the edges of the path $0,1,7,8,9,4,3,2,6,5$. This graph is Hamiltonian with $\delta=2$, and $R(G)$ has two components, with 18 and 4 paths. The graph6 codes of the 5 such graphs found in this family are in `data/hamiltonian_disconnected.txt`.

4. **A stronger fixed-endpoint property (new conjecture).** For a vertex $b$, let $R_b(G)$ be the graph on Hamiltonian paths with endpoint $b$, with backbites only at the other end (Pósa rotations). Then:

   - For every graph with $n\le10$ and $\delta\ge n/2$ (all 108,376 graphs at $n=10$), $R_b(G)$ is connected for every $b$.
   - The property fails for some graphs with $\delta=(n-1)/2$ ($n=7$: 9 graphs, $n=9$: 12 graphs).
   - Connectivity of all $R_b(G)$ implies connectivity of $R(G)$ for Hamiltonian $G$. Walk around a Hamiltonian cycle: $C-bb^+$ has endpoints $b$ and $b^+$.

   We conjecture that $\delta(G)\ge n/2$ implies $R_b(G)$ connected for every $b$. This would imply Conjecture 7.8.

5. **Closure fails.** "$R(G+uv)$ connected $\Rightarrow$ $R(G)$ connected whenever $d(u)+d(v)\ge n+1$" has no counterexample for $n\le 8$ but fails for $n=9$ (8 instances; `data/closure_n9.txt`). With threshold $n$ it already fails at $n=7$. So a Bondy–Chvátal-type closure argument does not work for general graphs.

6. **Forcing skeletons (property (F)).** The plain cycle $C_n$ is forcing for $n\le 9$ but not for $n=10$ (20 unreachable orderings) or $n=11$ (264). These graphs are forcing for all $n\le 12$ tested:

   - the ladder $P_m\square K_2$;
   - the prism $C_m\square K_2$;
   - the bipartite graph with edges $v_iv_{i+1},v_iv_{i+3}$;
   - its cyclic version;
   - the $3\times m$ grid.

   Instances of Lemma 2 were also verified to be forcing: $(1,2,3,3)$, $(1,2,4,4)$ and $(1,3,4,4)$.

7. **Diameter of $R(P_n^2)$.** Kazazi asks for the exact number of backbites needed. The exact diameters of $R(P_n^2)$ for $n=4,\dots,18$ are:

   $$3,3,5,6,7,8,10,11,12,14,15,16,18,19,21.$$
